Investor notes — initial landscape scan (October 2026)

103 companies across the four quadrants: 14 incumbents, 44 key players and 45 up-and-comers. 78 are privately held (use Ownership → “Private / venture-backed only” on the map). Coverage is US-centred, with leading European players included where they are shaping the model (Kraken, Tibber, Axle, GridBeyond, encentive). Below are the patterns that matter most for an impact allocator.

1. Flexibility is the thread running through every quadrant

  • Demand response and virtual power plants show up in all four quadrants. Thermostats (Everyday Electric, ecobee, Nest), home batteries (Tesla, Sunrun, Base, Lunar), C&I load (Enel, CPower, Voltus) and industry (GridBeyond, encentive) are all being sold as grid capacity, not just savings.
  • The value driver is shifting from “save energy” to “get paid to be flexible”. Revenue increasingly comes from capacity and ancillary markets and utility programs, so market-rule and regulatory risk is a core diligence item.

2. A consolidation wave is under way, so strategic exits are real

  • Recent deals: NRG–CPower (Jan 2026), Octopus majority stake in Uplight (Sep 2026), EnergyHub–Resideo Grid Services (Dec 2025), Trane–BrainBox AI (Jan 2025), Everyday Electric–Smartcar (Oct 2026), Arcadia–ENGIE Impact (announced May 2026), OPTERRA–NORESCO (Aug 2026), ABB–Lumin (Jan 2025).
  • The acquirers are utilities and retailers, HVAC OEMs and infrastructure investors. Independent scaled software platforms are becoming scarce, which supports valuations for the strongest remaining independents (Voltus, Leap, Camus, Texture).

3. Data-centre load is pulling capital to the C&I side, and can dilute the impact case

  • Voltus's “bring your own capacity” deals, Phaidra's AI cooling control, and on-site power providers ERock and Scale are all chasing hyperscaler demand.
  • Screen carefully: gas-based microgrids improve reliability and replace diesel, but they lock in fossil generation. Flexibility-first approaches have a cleaner additionality story.

3b. An AI-native software layer is forming around grid constraints

  • Making large loads flexible: Emerald AI ($150M Series A at a $1.05B valuation, Aug 2026), Hammerhead AI, Soma Energy and GridCARE help data centres connect sooner by agreeing to flex. Their impact depends on whether that flexibility actually displaces new gas plants.
  • Forecasting, trading and optimisation: Amperon, Ascend Analytics, Tyba, enspired, Modo Energy and Equilibrium Energy make storage and renewables bankable. Battery optimisation is getting crowded, so differentiation needs scrutiny.
  • Grid planning and hosting capacity: Neara, ThinkLabs AI, Gridsight, Rhizome and Nira Energy speed up interconnection and resilience planning. Utility sales cycles are long, but contracts are sticky once won.
  • Buildings, industry and fleets: Facilio, Grid Edge, Edo, etalytics, Gigaton, CVector, Companion.energy, Ampcontrol and BetterFleet are mostly at seed to Series B, which leaves room for impact capital to lead or co-lead.
  • Households and multifamily: Runwise and Parity (heating optimisation for apartments), Zero Homes and Nido (heat-pump quoting), Arbor (rate switching for renters), and Derapi and Beebop (VPP plumbing) target equity-relevant gaps.

4. The “-as-a-service” models need lots of capital, which suits blended finance

  • Base Power (~$2.3B raised), Budderfly ($550M+ equity plus debt), Redaptive (ABS and credit facilities), Sunrun and Haven all fund customer assets on their own balance sheets.
  • There is room for catalytic or junior debt, warehouse facilities and first-loss tranches, not just equity, especially where the customers are low-income households or small businesses.
  • Federal changes since 2025 have pushed residential solar and storage toward third-party ownership (lease, PPA and battery-as-a-service). That favours these models but raises cost-of-capital sensitivity.

5. Where the impact white space is

  • Renters and multifamily: few solutions beyond plug-in storage (Pila). Most home-energy tools assume an owner-occupier.
  • Low-income households: Haven (no-cost batteries for income-qualified California homes) and Rewiring America (a non-profit) are rare examples. BlocPower's 2026 wind-down is a cautionary data point on the unit economics of affordable-housing electrification.
  • Small commercial buildings (about 90% of US commercial buildings): GridPoint, 75F and Budderfly target the segment that traditional building-management systems and ESCOs ignore.
  • Rural co-ops and public power: Camus and Texture bring grid-edge software to under-resourced utilities.
  • Heat electrification and installation capacity: Quilt, Jetson and Bedrock Energy aim at the installer bottleneck and the cost of heat pumps and geothermal.

6. Watch-list and risks

  • Hardware cyclicality and margin pressure: Enphase layoffs, and Stem's difficult pivot to software.
  • Ownership changes that may shift strategy: sonnen (sold by Shell to Tiven, Aug 2026), Uplight (now led by Octopus), Kraken (spin-out from Octopus).
  • Interoperability “picks and shovels” such as Enode, Leap and Smartcar (now inside Everyday Electric) could become chokepoints as VPPs scale.

Method & sources

Compiled 11 Oct 2026 (a second pass the same day added 41 privately held, software-first companies) from company websites, SEC filings and annual reports, and press releases, plus trade and business press (Latitude Media, Canary Media, Utility Dive, TechCrunch, pv magazine, Business Wire, Reuters/Bloomberg). Private-company headcount and funding draw on aggregator estimates (CB Insights, PitchBook, LinkedIn-type data). Treat them as directional, not exact. Every company's popup links its sources, and caveats are flagged as a “data note”. Excluded as defunct or merged: BlocPower (winding down), Lumin (ABB), Turntide (pivoted to transport).   1. Create a new Google Sheet, e.g. “EMS Market Map — Team”. (Using a personal Gmail account is simplest; some university Google accounts block the “Anyone” access needed in step 4.)
  2. In the sheet, open Extensions → Apps Script. Delete the sample code and paste the dashboard's script:
  3. In the pasted code, change TEAM_PASSCODE = 'change-me' to a passcode for your group, then click Save.
  4. Click Deploy → New deployment, choose type Web app, set Execute as = Me and Who has access = Anyone, then Deploy. Approve the permissions prompt (if Google warns the app is unverified, choose Advanced → Go to project; it's your own script).
  5. Copy the Web app URL (ends in /exec), paste it above with your passcode, and click Connect. When asked, upload the current map so the sheet starts with all the research.
  6. Share the dashboard link plus the passcode with your group (send the passcode privately). Once the URL is built into the published page, they only need the passcode, and only if they want to make edits.

How to read & maintain the map

  • Position. Each company has an x score (0 = residential, 10 = utility / industrial) and a y score (0 = hardware & on-site services, 10 = pure software). The 5.0 lines split the quadrants. Bubbles are nudged slightly so labels don't collide; exact scores are in the data tab.
  • Size. Bubble area reflects the chosen metric (employees by default). Log scale keeps start-ups visible next to multinationals; switch to “proportional area” to see true relative scale. Hollow bubbles = no data for that metric.
  • Colour. Colour encodes the firm's role in the landscape (incumbent, key player, up-and-comer); stage of financing is in the popup and filter.
  • Conglomerates. Headcount and revenue for large incumbents are whole-company figures unless the popup says otherwise, so they overstate the energy-management business specifically.
  • Research pipeline. Log promising names in the To Research tab; “Add to map →” opens the company form pre-filled and marks the item done when you save.
  • Updating. Use + Add company (map or data tab), click a bubble → Edit, or bulk-import a CSV. Then Download updated dashboard to keep a permanent copy.
